Consistent with BGN, we show empirically that classification of firms to MVE and B/M portfolios as in Fama and French (1992, 1993) is conditioned on recent growth in capital expenditures. Fama and French (1992, 1993) observe size and book-to-market (B/M) effects in the cross-section of stock returns and identify factors based on market value of equity (MVE) and B/M that explain returns over time. Size and book-to-market, in general, and the Fama-French factor model, in particular, are now routinely used to benchmark returns in studies of long-run stock performance. For example, recent studies report that anomalies such as the "new issues puzzle" attenuate when benchmarks incorporate MVE and B/M (Brav, Geczy, and Gompers, 2000).

Whether observed B/M and size effects as distinguished by Fama and French (FF) result from an equilibrium asset-pricing model remains controversial, however. On one hand, similar findings have been reported in samples varying by country (Chan, Hamao, and Lakonishok, 1991; Chen and Zang, 1998; Fama and French, 1998) and firm type (Barber and Lyon, 1997). On the other hand, alternative explanations for the FF results include biases attributable to data snooping or data sources (Black, 1993; Lo and Mackinlay, 1990; Kothari, Shanken, and Sloan, 1995), risk premia for omitted state

Although data source biases appear not to be so severe as to be solely responsible for the results (Davis, 1994; Chan, Jegadeesh, and Lakonishok, 1995), the evidence on the size and B/M effects appears to be sensitive to the treatment of extreme observations (Knez, and Ready, 1997), period under study (Hawawini and Keim, 1995) and the influence of young growth stocks (Loughran, 1997). Additionally, although the return premiums associated with market value variables are detectable in many international stock markets, they are not correlated across markets (Hawawini and Keim, 1998). Also, country-specific versions of the Fama-French factor model explain time-series variation in international stock returns better than the world factor model (Griffin, 2002).

Recently, Berk, Green, and Naik (BGN, 1999) develop a model that directly relates risk and return to firm-specific characteristics such as capital investment, firm size, and book-to-market. In the model, the value of the firm is equal to the value of assets in place and growth options. Each period, firms find new investment opportunities, make investment decisions, and lose exhausted assets in place. Investment opportunities with low systematic risk are attractive to the firm and lead to large increases in market value, ceteris paribus. Making such investments reduces the average systematic risk of the firm’s cash flows in subsequent periods, which on average leads to lower realized returns. Book-to-market value of equity therefore summarizes the firm’s risk relative to the scale of the asset base. Changes in the firm’s asset portfolio over its life cycle also lead to an explanatory role for market value because such changes alter the relative importance of growth options versus existing assets within the firm. Firms with higher market value tend to have relatively more assets in place and greater current cash flows. In short, the model develops implications of the exercise of real investment options for the dynamics of returns and risk across firms that are related to observable firm-specific characteristics such as MVE and B/M.

The model of BGN contains several empirical implications, but BGN rely on simulations to demonstrate implications of their model and do not investigate actual firm-level data. In this paper we empirically investigate the implications of the BGN model,

## I. Capital Investment and the Evolution of Firm-specific Characteristics

In this section we investigate the extent to which firm-level investment conditions other firm-specific characteristics such as B/M and size. The model of BGN implies that firms that undertake new investments subsequently experience changes in size and book-to-market that are also related to the size and B/M effects in the cross-section of expected stock returns. To detect these relations, we rely on stock price and return data from the Center for Research on Securities Prices (CRSP) and financial statement data from COMPUSTAT over the period 1976 to 1999. We do not include firms until they are on the COMPUSTAT database for three years to reduce the survival biases. In addition, in computing returns we require 36 months of data before a company is included in a portfolio. These requirements should reduce the influence of small, young growth stocks on the results (Loughran, 1997). Finally, only non-financial firms (SIC other than in the 60s) and firms with ordinary common equity (security type 10 or 11 in CRSP) are considered. Throughout our analysis we use the firm classification and portfolio formation methods of Fama and French (1992, 1993), adapted when necessary for variables based on firm-level capital expenditures.$^{1}$ Our sample period differs from Fama and French (1992, 1993), but in an appendix available from the authors we show that the results from their sample period are present in our sample period, too.

## II. Capital Investment and Subsequent Stock Returns

In this section we investigate whether firm-level investment conditions the cross section of observed stock returns. Most studies that look at the association between investment and returns use aggregate macroeconomic data and examine the determinants of investment (Barro, 1990; Blanchard, Rhee, and Summers, 1993). We look at investment of individual firms because we seek to explain the size and B/M premiums by considering corporate investment, and most of the evidence on such premiums comes from firm-level data. Incidentally, we also employ the standard portfolio-based methodology common in the literature on market “anomalies” so that our findings can be directly compared to the results of that literature.

### III. Capital Investment and Factor Models of Returns

Subsequent to observing size and B/M effects in the cross-section of stock returns, Fama and French (1993) show that the market factor (MKT), a portfolio long in small stocks and short in big stocks (SMB), and a portfolio long in high B/M stocks and short in low-B/M stocks (HML) significantly explain average returns on portfolios formed on the basis of MVE and B/M. Fama and French (1996) argue that this three-factor model approximates an equilibrium asset-pricing model. In contrast, Daniel and Titman (1997) suggest that firm-specific characteristics themselves, i.e., size and B/M, appear to explain the variation in stock returns rather than the covariance structure of returns among factors derived from characteristic-intensive portfolios. Relatedly, Ferson, Sarkissian, and Simin (1999) recommend caution when using portfolios based on an observed relation between stock returns and a firm characteristic to test factor models based on such characteristics. Specifically, they show that the returns of characteristic-sorted portfolios may appear to be useful factors even when the characteristics are not related to risk.

The model of BGN is consistent with a characteristic-based explanation for empirical relations between stock returns and firm-specific characteristics such as size, B/M, and investment. A characteristic effect in the cross-section of returns will also support a factor model based on the characteristic, however (Ferson, Sarkissian, and Simin, 1999). Consequently, in this section, we investigate the influence of capital investment in a factor-based model of returns. We are not necessarily arguing for an alternative factor-based model or for an additional factor derived from firm-specific corporate investment. Rather, we want to investigate two additional topics. First, we investigate whether the relation between corporate investment growth and subsequent stock returns is robust to the FF three-factor model. Second, we construct a return factor based on firm-level investment growth and show that this factor performs as we might predict. Specifically, we hypothesize that the returns of stocks sorted by corporate investment growth contain information about the dynamics of asset turnover and growth opportunities. We also

### A. A Capital Investment Return Factor

We construct a return factor based on growth in capital investment, which we refer to as INV. Each June we sort stocks into five groups based on past investment growth, defined as the average investment growth over the two years prior to portfolio formation. We next subtract the returns on the high investment group from the low investment group each month, starting in July. In this manner we construct a portfolio-based factor comparable to the HML and SMB factors constructed by Fama and French.$^{6}$

The mean monthly investment index (INV) return is 0.24% for our sample period. This is consistent with our earlier evidence showing that the returns of low investment firms tend to be higher than the returns of high investment stocks. Our sample period is 1976-1999 whereas the sample period of Fama and French is 1963-1991. The mean monthly market return in excess of the one-month Treasury bill rate is 0.75% for our sample period, higher than that reported by FF (0.43%). In contrast, means for SMB (0.12%) and HML (0.30%) are lower than those in FF (0.27% and 0.40%, respectively), reflecting attenuation of observable size and B/M effects in the 1990s. In addition, HML is significantly correlated with the market premium (-0.51), with SMB (-0.22) and with INV (0.38). ## IV. Summary and Discussion

We examine the empirical associations among firm-level capital investment, market value of equity (MVE), the book-to-market ratio (B/M), and stock returns. Berk, Green, and Naik (BGN, 1999) develop a model that relates exercise of growth options to the evolution of fundamentals such as MVE and B/M, exposure to systematic risk, and expected stock returns. Specifically, the model of BGN implies that capital investment tends to increase MVE, decrease B/M, decrease risk, and lower expected stock returns. Our analysis tests each of these predictions using firm-level data.

We first show that stocks of firms classified as low-B/M by the widely used Fama and French (FF) methods significantly accelerate capital investment prior to the classification year. Their market values increase and their leverage levels diminish. Stocks of firms classified as high-B/M tend to reduce investment and increase their market leverage. In short, FF classification and portfolio formation methods are implicitly conditioned by recent firm-level investment patterns in a manner consistent with the predictions of BGN.

Second, we form portfolios based on growth in capital expenditures and find that average returns are significantly lower for portfolios composed of firms that have recently accelerated investment spending. Within investment growth groups, evidence of a value premium is weakened. For example, in the highest investment growth group the returns of small and high-B/M (S/H) versus big and low-B/M (B/L) portfolios, and small and low-B/M (S/L) versus big and high-B/M (B/H) portfolios are not significantly different, particularly after the month of January is excluded. Prior growth in capital expenditures is also highly significant in cross-sectional (Fama-MacBeth) regressions and appears to contain information similar to that of B/M.

Third, we investigate the performance of a pricing factor based on investment growth to explain returns on portfolios over time. When firms are assigned to portfolios based on investment growth we find that such a factor performs better than the SMB and HML factors of Fama and French. The investment factor also can substitute for the SMB factor in explaining returns on B/M-sorted portfolios. We do not suggest that these results necessarily support the need for new specifications of factor models. Rather, these results are consistent with the notion that a relation between firm-specific characteristics and expected returns will also be reflected in empirical support for a factor-based model in which factors are constructed on the basis of such characteristics (Ferson, Sarkission, and Simin, 1999).

Overall, the evidence is consistent with the model of Berk, Green, and Naik (1999) and in many ways complements existing interpretations of B/M and size as firm-specific characteristics related to risk. Specifically, Fama and French (1995) find that the profitability of low-B/M stocks increases prior to portfolio formation and subsequently decreases, while the opposite is true for high-B/M stock. They conjecture that the average return on capital of low-B/M firms increases prior to portfolio formation (i.e., they find some profitable investment opportunities) and thus they expand investment and output until marginal earnings return to competitive-equilibrium levels. In contrast, high-B/M stocks’ average return on capital decreases prior to portfolio formation and so these firms contract investment until earnings return to equilibrium levels.

Our findings suggest several avenues for future research. First, Titman, Wei, and Xie (2001) also report a negative association between capital investment and stock returns after benchmarking for factors such as size and B/M, but they interpret this as evidence of widespread agency problems of over-investment.$^{7}$ The model of BGN, in contrast, predicts the inverse relation between firm-level investment and stock returns, a relation that our empirical analysis of the relations among investment and size and B/M appears to support. Nevertheless, whether over-investment is partially responsible for the observed relation between investment and stock returns should be subject to additional investigation.

Second, the robustness of long-run return anomalies to size and B/M benchmarks has generated substantial controversy (Fama, 1998; Loughran and Ritter, 2000). Our results suggest that in some event-study applications it may prove useful to benchmark long-run returns for firm-level investment patterns. For example, many studies examine long-run returns to firms subsequent to new security offerings and report negative abnormal returns. Benchmarking long-run returns to changes in investment spending that may coincide with financing events might attenuate abnormal returns.

Third, the international evidence on the three-factor model of FF is mixed. Specifically, country-specific versions of the Fama-French factor model perform better than a global factor model (Griffin, 2002). If changes in investment opportunity sets and corporate investment activity are imperfectly correlated across countries then expected stock returns should demonstrate country-specific trends under the BGN model. In particular, country-specific patterns in capital investment might help to explain country-specific patterns in stock returns.

Finally, empirical tests appear to support asset-pricing models that employ variables derived from aggregate, macroeconomic data on corporate investment (Cochrane, 1991, 1996; Li, Vassalou, and Xing, 2001). Investigation of the relations between such models and specifications based on factors derived from firm-specific characteristics such as MVE, B/M, and capital investment seems a fruitful area for future research.
